Abstract
Vision-language pretraining objectives differ in the spatial
granularity of their supervision, yet the implications of this
distribution for chest X-ray interpretation remain underexplored. We present a controlled study that isolates the pretraining objective: holding the encoder and pretraining data
fixed, we train nine objectives spanning global and local contrastive learning, captioning, and their combinations, and evaluate across five chest X-ray tasks of increasing spatial granularity. We find that (i) pretraining granularity aligns with task
granularity at the extremes, with local objectives leading on
abnormality detection and global objectives on classification;
(ii) local objectives are surprisingly competitive on globallevel generation and question-answering tasks; (iii) the merits
of captioning and contrastive learning reverse across granularity levels; and (iv) among combinations, mixing captioning
and contrastive supervision is strongest on classification and
in-distribution generation, while pairing two captioning objectives generalizes best on zero-shot report generation. These
results show that no single objective is universally optimal,
and that the interaction of objective type, granularity, and task
governs downstream performance.

Introduction
Chest X-rays are the most widely used medical imaging procedure worldwide, valued for their accessibility and ease of
use (Rajpurkar et al. 2017). However, growing demand has
increased the workload on the limited number of radiologists
who interpret these images. Radiologists perform a diverse
range of tasks depending on the clinical context, from identifying abnormalities to localizing findings and generating
detailed diagnostic reports (Akhter, Singh, and Vatsa 2023).
Machine learning models have shown considerable promise
in chest X-ray interpretation, offering an opportunity to alleviate this burden. This is especially valuable in low-resource
settings, where radiologist shortages are most acute.
Despite impressive benchmark results, clinical deployment of these models remains limited (Dutta et al. 2025).
Medical vision-language models typically employ frozen vision encoders pretrained with contrastive learning (Radford
et al. 2021), applying the same representations across all
chest X-ray interpretation tasks. A fundamental challenge is
that these encoders often fail to extract sufficiently rich and
task-relevant representations (Tong et al. 2024). We argue

Chest X-ray interpretation tasks exhibit substantial variation in their granularity requirements, which can be arranged
along a spectrum of spatial granularity as shown in Figure
1. At one extreme, global-level tasks such as disease classification rely on high-level semantic understanding of the
entire image. At the other end, local-level tasks such as abnormality detection and phrase grounding require spatiallygrounded representations that focus on specific anatomical
regions. Other tasks, such as report generation, visual question answering (VQA), and Medical Difference VQA (Hu
et al. 2023b) fall between the two extremes, requiring both
global and local representations in varying degrees.
Vision encoders are pretrained using vision-language objectives that vary along the same axis. Global objectives,
such as image-text contrastive learning (Radford et al. 2021)
and image captioning (Tschannen et al. 2023), incentivize
learning coarse semantic representations aligned with global
image content. While effective for high-level understanding
tasks, these objectives provide limited supervision for learning spatially precise visual representations. In contrast, local objectives, such as referring expression comprehension,
phrase grounding, and local contrastive learning, incorporate
region-level supervision that explicitly aligns regional captions with local image regions, resulting in more spatially
grounded representations (Wan et al. 2024). Despite this
parallel, the extent to which pretraining granularity affects
downstream performance across the task spectrum remains
underexplored
In this work, we systematically investigate a fundamental question: Do local vision-language pretraining objectives learn visual representations that are better suited
for spatially grounded chest X-ray interpretation than
global objectives, and does this advantage increase as
tasks demand finer spatial granularity? To answer this
question, we perform a controlled empirical study in which
we hold the vision encoder architecture fixed and vary only
the pretraining objective. We evaluate the resulting representations across five downstream chest X-ray interpretation
tasks: classification, report generation, VQA, medical difference VQA, and abnormality detection. By isolating the
pretraining objective as the only experimental variable, we

Our results support the hypothesis that localized pretraining benefits spatially-grounded tasks, with local objectives
consistently outperforming global ones on abnormality detection, and global objectives edging out local ones on classification. However, the relationship between pretraining granularity and task granularity on tasks with intermediate granularity requirements is more nuanced. Local objectives are
competitive on global-level tasks, outperforming global objectives on standard natural language metrics for report generation and visual question answering, while global captioning retains an advantage on clinically grounded RadGraph-F1
evaluation. We further find that the relative merits of captioning versus contrastive learning depend on the granularity
level at which the objective is defined. Global captioning
outperforms global contrastive learning, while local contrastive learning outperforms local captioning across most
tasks. Among combined objectives, Local Contrastive and
Global Captioning and Local Contrastive and Global Contrastive emerge as the strongest, with local contrastive learning proving to be a uniquely non-redundant signal whose
presence consistently distinguishes the strongest combinations from the weakest.

Related Work
Vision-Language Pretraining
Traditionally, vision encoders were trained using supervised
learning, which requires large amounts of manually annotated data and is therefore costly to scale. Inspired by the
success of self-supervised learning in natural language processing, computer vision increasingly shifted toward leveraging large collections of image-text pairs available on the web.

This shift motivated the development of vision-language pretraining methods that learn aligned representations of images
and text. Early approaches, such as CLIP (Radford et al.
2021), demonstrated that contrastive learning can produce
vision encoders whose performance rivals that of supervised
models. Subsequent methods, including SigLIP (Zhai et al.
2023), further validated the effectiveness of image-text alignment objectives. Generative objectives based on image captioning (Tschannen et al. 2023) have since emerged as a
strong alternative to contrastive pretraining, particularly for
multimodal downstream tasks. More recent work (Xiao et al.
2024; Wan et al. 2024) has explored combining multiple pretraining objectives to capture information at different levels
of semantic granularity within a shared vision encoder.

Global and Localized Vision-Language Alignment
Global alignment objectives (Radford et al. 2021; Zhai et al.
2023; Tschannen et al. 2023) learn correspondences between
entire images and their associated reports or captions. Methods such as contrastive learning (Radford et al. 2021) and image captioning (Tschannen et al. 2023) operate at the image
level, encouraging the model to learn semantic representations that capture the overall content of an image. While these
approaches have demonstrated strong performance across a
variety of downstream tasks, they do not explicitly model
the relationship between specific image regions and textual
descriptions.
Local alignment objectives (Wan et al. 2024) instead induce correspondences between image regions and localized textual references, through tasks such as automatic
referring expression comprehension, grounded captioning,
and region-level contrastive learning. By providing explicit
spatial supervision, these objectives encourage spatially
grounded representations that may be better suited to lo-

calization and fine-grained reasoning. Recent work (Huang
et al. 2021) has further shown that combining global and
local objectives improves performance on multimodal tasks,
suggesting that the spatial granularity of the pretraining signal plays an important role in the quality of the learned
representations.

Local Alignment Objectives
Local Captioning We adopt a set of regional captioning
tasks to learn local correspondences between text and image
regions. Regions are defined by anatomical structures in the
chest, such as the mediastinum and left lung, and each is
represented by a bounding box paired with a caption. From
these annotations, we formulate three local vision-language
tasks. Grounded Captioning (GCAP) generates a regional
caption given an image and a bounding box. Automatic Referring Expression (AREF) predicts a bounding box given
an image and a regional caption. Conditional Automatic Referring Expression (CAREF) generates both a caption and a
bounding box given an image and the anatomical name of a
region. We train the model on all three tasks jointly in a multitask setup (Beyer et al. 2023). Each task is formulated as an
autoregressive sequence generation problem and optimized
with the objective in Equation 3, with a task-specific prompt
prepended to indicate the desired task and the loss computed
over the generated tokens only. The decoder follows the same
setup as global captioning.
Local Contrastive We extend contrastive learning from
the image-report level to the region-caption level. Instead
of aligning entire chest X-ray images with reports, we align
anatomically defined image regions with their corresponding
phrases: for each phrase, we contrast the ViT patch tokens
within its bounding-box region against the phrase embedding. Regions that share the same phrase text and anatomical name are treated as positive pairs, while all remaining
region-phrase combinations are treated as negatives. We optimize this objective with the same formulation, Transformer
encoder, and training setup as global contrastive learning,
differing only in the granularity of the aligned pairs.

Experiment
Task Setup
We use MIMIC-CXR (Johnson et al. 2019) for visionlanguage pretraining, retaining only PA and AP views, excluding studies missing a findings or impression section,
and removing references to prior imaging. After filtering,
242,575 studies remain across the official splits. We partition the training set into two disjoint subsets: 100,000 studies
for pretraining and 99,341 for downstream training of report
generation, VQA, and medical difference VQA. Images are
resized to 384 × 384 and converted to RGB. In all downstream experiments, we freeze the encoder and train only
a task-specific head, isolating the effect of the pretraining
objective.
For objectives requiring local supervision, we use Chest
ImaGenome (Wu et al. 2021), which augments MIMIC-CXR
with region-level bounding boxes and report phrases. Since
identical phrases often map to multiple regions, we assign
each phrase to a single anatomical structure, choosing the
most specific region when nested regions apply; negative
findings are assigned to the region where the pathology
would typically manifest. Bounding boxes are represented as
[xmin , ymin , xmax , ymax ], normalized by image dimensions
and scaled by 1000.
All models use the CLIP ViT-L/14 (Radford et al. 2021)
vision encoder, trained from scratch alongside a text module
instantiated as a bidirectional Transformer encoder for contrastive objectives or an autoregressive decoder for captioning objectives. Both text module variants use 6 layers, 8 attention heads, hidden dimension 512, feed-forward dimension

2048, and dropout 0.1, with the GPT-2 tokenizer (50,257 tokens), learned positional embeddings, and a learned [CLS]
token for sequence-level representations. Models are trained
for 50 epochs with AdamW, a learning rate of 1 × 10−4 , and
cosine annealing; we select the checkpoint with the lowest
validation loss.
The resulting encoders are evaluated on six downstream
tasks spanning the granularity spectrum introduced in the
Introduction Section, each described in the following subsections. The performance for all generative tasks is measured using BLEU-1/4, ROUGE-1/L, RadGraph-F1, and
BERTScore. The performance for classification is measured
using AUC while that of abnormality detection is measured using mAP@0.4 and mAP@0.5. We report the mean
and standard deviation over three seeds. We perform paired
bootstrap resampling (Berg-Kirkpatrick, Burkett, and Klein
2012) with Benjaminin-Hochberg FDR correction (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995) across all pairwise comparisons;
full results are presented in the technical supplement.
Multilabel Classification Our primary evaluation protocol for classification is linear probing. In this setting, a single
linear layer is trained on top of visual representations extracted from the vision encoder using binary cross-entropy
(BCE) loss with SGD optimizer, a learning rate of 0.1, cosine
annealing scheduler, a batch size of 512, and for 30 epochs.
We utilize the NIH CXR-14 dataset (?), a 14-class multi-label
dataset comprising 94,596 chest X-ray images from 30,805
patients. We strictly follow the official train and test splits.

Visual Question Answering We train a text decoder on
the frozen vision features to answer questions about chest
X-ray images, using the single-image subset of the Medical
Difference VQA dataset (Hu et al. 2023a). The questions
span six categories — location, presence, abnormality, type,
severity, and view — with 202,982 training and 25,689 test
questions. To avoid data leakage, we retain only questions
whose images were excluded from pretraining. The decoder
architecture is described under Task Setup: at each layer,
masked self-attention models the question and previously
generated answer tokens, while cross-attention integrates the
visual features. It is trained from scratch with cross-entropy
loss, using AdamW for 10 epochs with a learning rate of
1 × 10−4 , batch size 64, and cosine annealing with a 5%
linear warm-up.
Medical Difference VQA We train a text decoder on the
frozen vision features to describe longitudinal changes between a pair of chest X-rays acquired from the same patient
at different time points. We use the “difference” subset of the
Medical Difference VQA dataset, comprising 104,066 training, 12,934 validation, and 12,918 test questions. To prevent
data leakage, we ensure that neither image in any test pair
appears in the pretraining dataset. The frozen features from
the two images are concatenated into a single cross-attention
memory, with learned frame embeddings added to indicate
whether each token originates from the reference or current
image. In each decoder layer, masked self-attention models
the question and previously generated answer tokens, while
cross-attention integrates information from both images. The
decoder architecture is described under Task Setup. It is
trained from scratch with cross-entropy loss, using AdamW
for 20 epochs with a learning rate of 1 × 10−4 , weight decay
0.01, batch size 20, and cosine annealing.

Rather than a single objective emerging as a universal winner, the results reveal a nuanced interplay between objective
type, granularity level, and task characteristics.

Pretraining granularity aligns with task
granularity at the extremes
The clearest support for the granularity hypothesis comes
from the two tasks at opposite ends of the spatial granularity
spectrum. On abnormality detection (VinDr-CXR, Figure 3),
the most spatially demanding task, localized objectives outperform global ones: Local Contrastive (6.56 mAP@0.4)
and Local Captioning (5.64) surpass all global single objectives, the best being Global Sigmoid (5.02). Conversely, on
classification (NIH-CXR-14, Figure 2), a global task, global
objectives win: Global Contrastive leads among single objectives (65.3 AUC), with Global Sigmoid (65.0) and Global
Captioning (64.5) also surpassing the best local objective,
Local Contrastive (63.5). This alignment, with local objectives winning on the localized task and global objectives on
the global one, is consistent with the hypothesis that pretraining granularity should match task granularity. Two nuances stand out. On detection, the local advantage is strongest
for Local Contrastive, while Local Captioning’s margin over
Global Sigmoid is modest. Within the global objectives on
detection, Global Sigmoid substantially outperforms Global
Contrastive (2.69) and Global Captioning (3.82), suggesting
its pairwise binary loss provides a finer-grained signal than
InfoNCE even at the image level. Detection and classification
thus offer complementary evidence for the hypothesis at the
two extremes; the intermediate tasks, examined next, reveal
a more complex picture.

Captioning and contrastive learning excel at
different granularity levels
Within the global category, captioning outperforms contrastive on most tasks, and the MIMIC-CXR significance
results confirm that Global Captioning is significantly better
than both Global Contrastive and Global Sigmoid across all
metrics. Within the local category, the pattern reverses: Local Contrastive outperforms Local Captioning on most tasks,
including detection (Figure 3),classification (Figure 2), VQA
(Table 2), and difference VQA (Table 3), and is competitive
or leading on all three report generation datasets (Tables 1).
This interaction suggests that the relative merits of captioning
and contrastive learning cannot be assessed independently of
the spatial scale at which they are applied.

Mixing captioning and contrastive objectives yields
the strongest combinations
On classification and in-distribution generation, combinations mixing captioning and contrastive are strongest. Local
contrastive and global captioning leads on NLG metrics for
MIMIC-CXR report generation (Table 1), VQA (Table 2),
and medical difference VQA (Table 3), and its mirror image,
Local captioning and global contrastive, is similarly strong,
indicating that the benefit steps from mixing supervision
types rather than the granularity assignment. On classification, the combinations with global contrastive learning lead
with a considerable margin over single objective encoders
underscoring the contrastive component’s importance for
discriminative tasks. Local captioning and global captioning departs from this pattern: it is worst on classification and
on MIMIC-CXR RadGraph, yet strongest among combinations on the zero-shot IU X-ray and CheXpert Plus report
generation datasets, revealing that pure captioning supervision generalizes best out-of-distribution even when it under
performs in-distribution.

Limitations
Our findings are scoped to the frozen-encoder transfer setting
and may not hold under full finetuning, which could alter the
relative standing of the objectives. We evaluate a single architecture (CLIP ViT-L/14) at one pretraining scale, and most
intermediate tasks derive from MIMIC-CXR, limiting out-

of-distribution evidence. Finally, the local objectives require
dense region-level annotations (bounding boxes paired with
phrases), which are costly to obtain and unavailable for most
datasets and imaging modalities, constraining the settings in
which localized pretraining is practical.

Conclusion
We investigated how pretraining objectives affect five chest
X-ray interpretation tasks spanning two levels of granularity.
Our results partially support the hypothesis that granularitymatched pretraining helps: localized objectives lead on abnormality detection and global objectives on classification.
The picture is more complex on intermediate tasks, where
local objectives are competitive on report generation and
VQA, outperforming global objectives on NLG metrics
while global captioning retains an edge on RadGraph-F1.
The merits of captioning versus contrastive learning also depend on granularity, with global captioning beating global
contrastive but local contrastive beating local captioning
across most tasks. Among combinations, mixing captioning and contrastive supervision is strongest on classification
and in-distribution generation, while two captioning objectives generalize best on zero-shot report generation. These
findings challenge the assumption that pretraining granularity maps cleanly onto task requirements, pointing instead to
the interaction of objective type, granularity, and task as the
primary determinant of downstream performance.
